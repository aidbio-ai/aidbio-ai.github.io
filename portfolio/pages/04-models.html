Chapter 04 · Models

Predicting a regulatory edge before confirming it at the bench.

The most recent research step reframes the same kind of question — "what regulates what?" — as a prediction problem: propose a specific regulatory interaction computationally, then test it experimentally.

A concrete predicted interaction

Does a microRNA control the stability of an ABA-receptor transcript?

Work on the ABA receptor family (PYR/PYL/RCAR) asked whether receptor transcript levels are shaped not only by transcription, but by targeted mRNA decay. Sequence and expression evidence pointed to microRNA5628 as a candidate regulator of the PYL6 transcript — a specific predicted edge in the post-transcriptional regulatory network, later supported by decay-assay evidence involving the XRN4 exoribonuclease pathway.

Structurally, this is the same problem class as the network chapter, but posed prospectively: instead of describing a hub after the fact, propose a specific edge and go test whether it holds.

From one predicted edge to graph-scale prediction

The same logic, applied at the scale of a whole graph

2011 → 2024

From integrating two signals at one gene, to predicting one regulatory edge from sequence and expression evidence — the unit of prediction stayed small, but the reasoning became computational.

Today, at AidBio

That same prediction logic — propose an edge, weigh the evidence, flag it for confirmation — is what AidBio's graph-learning tool, Aid2GO, runs at the scale of full protein–protein interaction and Gene Ontology graphs.

What stays constant

A prediction is not a confirmed result. Every computational claim in this progression is kept distinct from experimentally validated ones — a discipline carried forward into AidBio's software.
